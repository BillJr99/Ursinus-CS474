## Explore Further

These curated resources present sharply opposing positions — read at least two sides:

- [Bryson, J. - Robots Should Be Slaves](https://www.cs.bath.ac.uk/~jjb/ftp/Bryson-Slaves-Book09.html) — a deliberately provocative chapter arguing that robots are property we design, and that granting them rights misdirects moral concern away from humans.  Compare its reasoning with the Birhane & van Dijk paper from the supplemental reading.
- [European Parliament Resolution on Civil Law Rules on Robotics (2017)](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/TA-8-2017-0051_EN.html) — the real legislative document that floated "electronic personhood" for sophisticated robots (see paragraph 59(f)).  Notice that the motivation is largely about *liability* — who pays when a robot harms someone — rather than robot welfare.
- [Darling, K. - Extending Legal Protection to Social Robots (2012)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2044797) — argues from human psychology: because people anthropomorphize social robots, mistreating them may harm *us*, paralleling animal-cruelty law reasoning.
- [Boston Dynamics - Atlas robot demonstrations](https://www.youtube.com/@BostonDynamics) (video channel) — watch any recent Atlas or Spot video and observe your own emotional reaction when a robot is pushed or falls.  Written takeaway: our empathy responds to lifelike *motion*, not to any inner experience — which is exactly the design lever (and ethical hazard) Darling's paper analyzes.
